Method

How to keep an indigo vat

The one-page method that is pinned above our own vat. It is the same sheet that goes in the kit.

Indigo does not dissolve in water. To get it onto cloth you have to reduce it — take the oxygen out — which turns it a yellow-green and makes it soluble. The cloth comes out that colour and turns blue in the air as the pigment oxidises back. That is the whole trick, and everything below is housekeeping.

A vat is alive in the sense that it uses something up and can be fed. Ours have run for two years. A vat you started this morning will dye today and will be better in a week.

The proportions, for five litres

50 g natural indigo
100 g fructose
150 g calcium hydroxide
5 l water at 50 °C

Paste the indigo first. Add the fructose, then the lime.
Stir once, hard, then leave it alone for an hour.

Building one

  1. Paste the indigo

    A little warm water and a lot of patience. Lumps of dry indigo never dissolve; they sit on the bottom and stain whatever touches them.

  2. Add fructose, then lime

    In that order. The sugar is the reducing agent and the lime raises the pH so the sugar can work.

  3. Stir once and stop

    One firm stir to bring everything together, then leave it. Every stir after that is oxygen you will have to take out again.

  4. Wait for the flower

    An hour, warm. A coppery skin gathers on the surface and the liquid under it goes yellow-green. That is a vat.

Reference
50 °CThe temperature a fructose vat wants. Colder and it reduces slowly; hotter and the indigo starts to break down.
pH 10–11Below ten the reduction stalls. Above eleven you are dyeing in a caustic bath and wool will not forgive it.
1 minuteA dip. Longer does not make it darker; more dips do.
20 minutesBetween dips, in the air, so each layer oxidises before the next one goes on.
8 dipsWhat our heavy indigo linen gets, over two days.

Lime is caustic

Calcium hydroxide burns skin and eyes and it does it slowly enough that you notice afterwards. Gloves, and do not lean over the pot to look.

The green is not a mistake

If your cloth comes out green and stays green, the vat is working and the air has not reached it yet. Hang it up.

What goes wrong

The vat is blue, not yellow-green.

It has oxidised. Feed it — a spoon of fructose, warmth, and an hour — and do not stir it while you wait.

The colour rubs off on my hands.

Unfixed pigment sitting on the surface. Rinse in cold water until it runs clear, then wash once with a little soap.

One side is darker than the other.

The cloth was folded in the vat. Ours are hung from a rail for this reason, and even then the top of a long piece is a shade lighter than the bottom.

Can I dye wool in this?

Yes, at the lower end of the pH and the temperature. Wool in a hot alkaline bath goes to felt and there is no undoing it.
