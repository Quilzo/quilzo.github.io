Dyestuff

No synthetic dye

Plants, one insect we label as one, and iron. If a colour cannot be got that way, we do not sell that colour.

Scale

Nothing over ten metres

A bath that size can be watched. Past it you are managing a process instead of dyeing cloth, and the unevenness stops being a feature.

Labels

No secret recipes

Every label names the dyestuff, the mordant and the number of dips. The guide has the method we actually use.

Where it happens

Woven in Somerset, dyed in a railway arch

The cloth is woven eleven miles away on a loom that has been in the same family for three generations. We buy it undyed, by the roll, and it arrives smelling of flax.

The dyeing happens in an arch under the mainline where the temperature is steady and the noise is somebody else's problem. Two vats, four pots, one sink.

The six we keep going

  • Indigo

    A vat dye. Reduced to dissolve it, oxidised to fix it, and the only blue worth having.

  • Madder

    Root, lifted at three years. Apricot to brick depending on your water.

  • Weld

    The oldest cultivated yellow in Europe, and it stays where turmeric does not.

  • Walnut

    Husks from one tree in Wiltshire. Substantive, so no mordant at all.

  • Cochineal

    An insect dye. We say so on the label and in the listing, because it matters to some people.

  • Logwood

    A purple nothing else makes, that fades in a south window. Line a coat with it.

How an order runs

  1. You choose a length

    Everything is cut from a roll, so a metre and seven metres are the same amount of work.

  2. We check the bath date

    If your colour was dyed more than a season ago we say so, because the next bath will not match it exactly and pretending otherwise is how people end up with two sleeves.

  3. It is cut and posted

    Two days for anything in stock. Made-to-order is three weeks, which is one bath and the drying.
