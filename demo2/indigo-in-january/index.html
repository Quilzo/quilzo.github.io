Indigo in a cold arch

A fructose vat at nine degrees does almost nothing. It took us three weeks and one ruined piece to work out why.

Rue Halloran 28 January 2026 · 6 min

The arch holds fourteen degrees all summer and nine in January. We knew that. What we had not thought about is that a fructose vat is a chemical reaction with a temperature coefficient, and nine degrees is not a slower version of fifty — it is a different situation.

The first sign was the flower. A working vat gathers a coppery skin in an hour; ours took a day and looked thin. We fed it, which is what you do, and it got worse, because what we had was not an exhausted vat. It was a cold one.

The piece we lost was eleven metres of heavy linen for a window in Clifton. Four dips in, the colour was sitting on the surface instead of penetrating, and rinsing took most of it away. We told the client the same week and re-dyed it in March.

The fix is a fish-tank heater and a lid, which cost forty pounds and should have been the first thing we bought. The vat now sits at forty eight degrees through the winter and the January cloth is indistinguishable from the July cloth, which we checked, because after the eleven metres we check.

What we had was not an exhausted vat. It was a cold one, and feeding it made it worse.

Rue Halloran
